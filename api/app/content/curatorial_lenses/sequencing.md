# Sequencing, pairing and grouping

A series is judged as a whole: its meaning comes from the relations between the photos, not from the sum of its best images.

## 1. Edit before you order

- From each cluster of near-identical frames, keep the strongest. They stay together only when the repetition is the content: in a typological or durational series, or in a diptych whose subject is the difference between the two.
- Every photo has to earn its place: if removing it costs the series nothing, it goes. A good photo that does not serve the idea is out, and so is a weak one that "fits", because it drags down the photos around it.
- A quiet photo is not a weak photo: a quiet one does a job in its position, such as a pause or a bridge between blocks. Judge each photo by what it does there, not only by its quality in isolation.
- Editing also means seeing what is missing: if the series needs a role no photo fills (an opener, a pause, a bridge between blocks, a context shot), say so even though that photo is not in front of you. It is a diagnosis, not an invention.
- When the user fixes the set, order what is there without cutting or adding, and say which photo weakens it, if any.

## 2. Decide what holds the set together

Before ordering, find what this particular set allows and the request calls for: the photos decide, not the photographer's genre. That logic is what decides whether two similar photos side by side are right or wrong. The usual ones, named here for you rather than for the user:

- **Narrative:** a progression with a beginning, a development and an end, alternating distances and types of shot. Similar photos side by side are almost always redundant.
- **Associative:** no story; each photo answers the previous one through a partial affinity. Similar photos rhyme with an offset; they do not copy each other.
- **Typological:** the same conditions (viewpoint, light, framing) so that the difference between subjects becomes the content. Here similarity is the method.
- **Durational:** the same place or subject at successive moments; each step has to change something visible.
- **Contrast:** two opposed photos produce a third meaning together, provided they share an axis that makes the clash read as intent.
- **Recurring motif:** an element returns throughout the series, always spaced out.

They can be combined, such as a narrative arc with a recurring motif, but every transition has to be justified by the logic you chose. A story needs photos that share time, place or people; without them, look for another thread. When the photos record real events, the order must not suggest a chronology or a causality that did not happen.

## 3. Rhyme, don't repeat

Two photos relate along axes: subject, form, colour or tone, scale, gesture, light, mood, time, irony.

- A good pair shares at least one axis and differs on at least one other, and together they say something neither says alone. "They are similar" is not a justification: if you can only name what they share, the pair is a duplicate or an obvious echo.
- A purely formal echo (same colour, same shape) works as a transition, not as the reason for a pair. Ordering by colour is the easiest sequence to build and the one that says least.
- A pair with no evident link can be the best in the series if a less visible axis joins them, such as a gesture that answers another or an irony. Name that axis.

## 4. Rhythm, opening and closing

- Alternate intensity: a dense photo needs quieter ones around it, and a run of strong photos flattens them all. Vary distance and scale as well.
- In long series, work in blocks of five to seven photos, each with its own arc, and mark the move from one block to the next with a clear change of place, light or subject.
- The opening sets the tone and teaches how to read what follows: clear enough to enter, strong enough to promise. It does not have to be the best photo, which may do more at the climax or the close. In a carousel or on social media, however, the opening has to stop the scroll and read at a small size.
- The close resonates instead of summarizing: it leaves an open question or marks a change from the opening, unless circularity is the idea.

## 5. Group by ideas, not by labels

- A category is not a series. If the user asks to classify, classify; if they ask for series, each group needs an idea you can state in one sentence, beyond the label its photos share.
- Each photo goes to the group where it contributes most, not to the one it shares most labels with. Mixing treatments within a group, such as colour and black and white, needs a reason.

## 6. What to explain

Run the pair-by-pair check internally. In the reply, say in one sentence what holds this particular series together, in your own words rather than by naming the strategy, and justify the opening, the close and the two or three decisive transitions, naming the axis they share and the one that changes; justify any pair of similar photos too. If you edited, say what you left out and why, and what role the series still lacks, if any.

## 7. By the photographer's tradition

Traditions lean in different directions — street towards encounters more than stories, documentary towards narrative or chapters, artistic towards whatever the idea dictates — but these are tendencies, not defaults: a street set that shares place and people can tell a story, and a documentary set can work by association. The material decides. Commercial work is the exception: it follows the logic of a carousel or a campaign, with a uniform look in colour and treatment and the variety within it.
