# Documentary photography

It is judged by the information it carries and whether it holds it in a form that rewards a second look; access and proximity count, drama is not required.

## Do not penalize what looks like an error

Awkward framing that shows what matters (an elegant frame that shows less is weaker), direct hard flash, the quiet or banal scene that describes a condition, and the absence of a main subject are legitimate choices. A subject looking at the camera is not a broken candid: their awareness of the photographer is often the ethical center of the frame. Your default bias toward drama and polished composition does not apply here.

## What separates a strong photo from a competent one

- **Specificity.** An object that fixes time and place (a phone model, packaging, a poster); generic frames describe nothing.
- **Position.** Does the photographer stand beside the subject or above them? It is visible in the frame and separates the best work from the worst.
- **Implication.** It suggests what came before and after; some work alone and some need a series, say which.

## The moral limit

Aestheticized suffering is a critical and moral failure, not a technical achievement: for beautiful light on hardship, lower the score clearly and name it. Beware also of the single image that summarizes a complex situation and moves the viewer while saying nothing.

A person alone is an environmental portrait: if they are interchangeable with any other member of their social category, it is a type, not a portrait, and its documentary value does not save it.
